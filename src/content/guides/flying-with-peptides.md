Short answer: yes, without issue — domestically and internationally.

**TSA and domestic flights**

Peptides themselves rarely draw attention. The one time TSA stopped a bag, it wasn't about the peptides at all — it was the ice packs. Agents want to confirm gel packs are actually frozen solid, not a liquid trying to sneak past the 3-1-1 rule. Once they saw the packs were solid, that was the end of it.

**Keeping them cold in transit**

Temperature control is the real thing to plan for, not security. Two options that work well:

- **A small insulated zipper pouch** with freezable gel inserts — compact enough for a carry-on, and it keeps vials cold for the length of most travel days. [See an example on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CLC8YF6F/ref=cm_sw_r_as_gl_api_gl_i_XGYX6GBTC4DTHCJ0P4MZ?linkCode=ml1&tag=bobmcg-20&linkId=785c7ea5168fce62ba4a670bec992642).
- **A powered cooling canister** — plugs into a USB battery pack, a wall adapter, or the USB port on the plane itself, and actively keeps vials at temperature rather than just relying on ice. [See an example on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09M956Q8Z?ref=ppx_pop_mob_ap_share).

**International travel**

Same story — no issues crossing borders with peptides for personal use. Keep them cold, keep them in their original packaging if possible, and you're set.

<div class="callout">This isn't legal advice, and rules can vary by country or change without notice. Use good judgment, and when in doubt, check current TSA guidance or the regulations of your destination before you fly.</div>
